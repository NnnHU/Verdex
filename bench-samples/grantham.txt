Jeremy Grantham and the 1999 Internet Bubble: A Cautionary Tale of Being Right Early

In late 1999, the U.S. stock market reached its highest valuations in history —
more expensive than the peak before the 1929 crash. Jeremy Grantham, co-founder
of the asset management firm GMO, had spent years studying 28 historical bubbles
and reached a stark conclusion: every single one of them eventually burst. His
firm built its entire methodology on "mean reversion" — the principle that asset
prices, pushed to extremes by emotion, eventually return to their long-run fair
value. GMO did not use complex mathematical optimizers; they used a simple
"cheap-to-expensive" scale to set portfolio weights.

The problem: from 1997 onward, the market entered a mania. Growth stocks and
internet companies soared, while cheap value stocks were abandoned. Grantham's
quantitative models and historical research all pointed to the market being
dangerously overvalued. He refused to chase the bubble, keeping clients
underweight the soaring tech sector and even shorting the U.S. market with
roughly $10 million of his own money.

The cost was brutal and immediate. GMO's assets under management collapsed from
roughly $30 billion in 1997 to about $20 billion by early 2000, as clients
redeemed in frustration at lagging returns. The asset-allocation division lost
around 60% of its clients. One hospital endowment's investment committee chair
shouted at Grantham in a Boston meeting: "Stop talking to us about mean
reversion!" Grantham's reply was unyielding: "Without mean reversion, I have no
way to explain to you where we came from or where we are going."

What made the situation cruel was not just the firm's bleeding — it was that
Grantham was personally squeezed on two fronts. As clients fled, GMO's
management-fee revenue collapsed. At the same time, his personal short position
faced relentless margin calls as the market kept climbing. He was nearly wiped
out financially, even though his core thesis was correct.

The deepest lesson of the episode is an industry rule of thumb, often
attributed to Keynes: "The market can stay irrational longer than you can stay
solvent." Many clients privately agreed that Grantham was probably right, but
they feared for their own jobs more. In institutional investing, the real risk
is not losing money — it is "career risk," the danger of being fired for
underperforming peers. As Keynes also noted: "It is better to fail
conventionally than to succeed unconconventionally." Most clients preferred to
lose money alongside everyone else rather than stand alone and be wrong in
public.

Before the bubble's peak, a wave of legendary value investors fell. Julian
Robertson's Tiger Fund closed. Gary Brinson was forced out at UBS. Stan
Druckenmiller stepped down. Grantham survived in part because GMO was a private
partnership — not publicly listed, answerable to no shareholders each quarter.

In March 2000, the Nasdaq finally peaked and the internet bubble burst,
beginning a two-and-a-half-year bear market. The stubborn old man who had been
mocked, abandoned by clients, and driven to the edge of personal bankruptcy was
about to be vindicated. Mean reversion, it turned out, was gravity — slow but
inescapable.
